AI 101 for beginners

New to AI? This is a plain-English starter course: what it is, why businesses use it, how to ask it for what you want, and how to stay safe.

Time: about 45 to 60 minutes on your own, or use it with the facilitator guide for a group. No coding, no experience and no payment needed. Tool plans and limits change, so check each tool's official site for what is free today.

The 30-second version

AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are very fast assistants that predict useful text, images or answers from patterns they learned. They are great at first drafts, summaries and brainstorming. They can also be confidently wrong, so a person checks the result and stays responsible for it.

What AI actually is

Business view: think of a very fast new hire who has read a huge amount, never gets tired, but sometimes makes things up and does not know your company. You would give that person clear instructions, check their work and keep private files locked away. That is how to treat AI.

A little more technical: a chatbot is built on a large language model (LLM). During training it studied enormous amounts of text and learned which words tend to follow which. When you type a prompt, it predicts a helpful reply one piece at a time. It is not looking things up unless the tool has a search feature, and it does not truly "know" whether something is true. That is why it can produce a wrong answer in a very confident tone, called a hallucination.

Why businesses use it

  • Time: first drafts of emails, reports and ad copy in minutes instead of an hour.
  • Cost: small teams can do work that used to need an agency or an extra hire.
  • Sorting and summarizing: turn long documents, reviews or call notes into short summaries.
  • Ideas: get ten options fast, then pick and improve the best one.

How to write a good prompt

A weak prompt gets a weak answer. Use these five parts, in any order:

  1. Role: who should the AI act as? ("a friendly small-business marketing coach")
  2. Task: what exactly do you want? ("write three subject lines")
  3. Context: what does it need to know? (audience, goal, tone, anything it should avoid)
  4. Format: how should the answer look? ("a numbered list, under 100 words")
  5. Check: ask it to flag what to verify, or to ask you questions first.
Act as a friendly small-business marketing coach. Write three subject lines for an email announcing our Thursday workshop for college students. Keep each under 8 words, no hype, no emojis. Then tell me which one you would test first and why.

If the first answer is not right, just reply with what to change. Talking back and forth is normal and is how most people get good results.

Your first hour: four short exercises

Open any free AI chat tool. Use made-up or non-private information only. Copy a prompt, run it, then read the answer critically.

Exercise 1: Ask for a plain-English explanation (5 minutes)

Pick something you have always found confusing, such as a term from class or work.

Explain [topic] to me like I am new to it. Use one everyday example from a small business. Then give me three questions I could ask to check I understood.

Exercise 2: Turn messy notes into something clean (5 minutes)

Type or paste a few made-up or non-private bullet points. Do not use real client or personal details.

Turn these notes into a short, friendly follow-up email. Keep it under 120 words and end with one clear next step.

Notes:
- met Tuesday
- they liked the proposal
- need pricing by Friday

Exercise 3: Make it check itself (5 minutes)

Ask a factual question about something you already know well, so you can spot mistakes.

Answer this question, then list which parts of your answer I should double-check and why: [your question]

Exercise 4: Use AI as a coach, not a ghostwriter (5 minutes)

Paste a paragraph you wrote yourself.

Act as a friendly editor. Point out the three biggest improvements for clarity, and explain each one. Do not rewrite the whole paragraph.

Stay safe from day one

  • I did not paste client names, health details, account numbers or passwords.
  • I checked any fact, number or date I plan to rely on.
  • I read the result myself before sending it to anyone.
  • I know my school or workplace rules about AI use, and I follow them.

Check yourself

Try to answer each question first, then open it.

1. An AI chatbot gives you a confident answer with a statistic. What should you do before using it in a client email?

Best answer: Check the number against an official or original source.

AI can state wrong facts with total confidence (a hallucination). Confident tone is not evidence.

2. Which of these is the safest thing to paste into a free AI chat tool?

Best answer: A paragraph from your own draft with no names or private details.

Keep client names, health details, account numbers, passwords and anything confidential out unless your organization has approved the tool for it.

3. What is a prompt?

Best answer: The instruction and context you give the AI.

Better prompts say who the AI should act as, what the task is, the context, the format you want, and how to check it.

4. A tool writes a great-sounding product description. Who is responsible if it makes a false claim?

Best answer: You and your business, not the AI.

A person stays accountable for anything that goes to customers. Review it before it goes out.

5. Which job is AI usually best at today?

Best answer: A first draft, a summary or a brainstorm that a person then reviews.

AI is a fast assistant for drafts and sorting. It is weaker at precise facts, math without a calculator, and anything that needs real judgment.
