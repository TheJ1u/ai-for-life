Scheduled news watch

Make AI check the news for you on a schedule: free and paid options, copy-and-paste prompts, and safety rules.

Club guide

Quick demo

What it is

A scheduled task is an alarm clock for an AI. Instead of you opening a chat each morning and asking "what is new in AI?", you write the request once and the AI runs it by itself at a set time, then leaves you a summary. Think of it as a personal research assistant who reads the news before you wake up.

Why people use it

  • Keeps you current on a topic without spending an hour scrolling.
  • Businesses use the same idea for competitor monitoring, regulation tracking and market news.
  • It turns one good prompt into a repeating habit. This is the simplest form of an AI workflow.

Pick your tool

  • Google Alerts (free, no AI): at google.com/alerts, enter a keyword and Google emails you new web results daily or weekly. Good for brand and name tracking. It lists links but does not summarize.
  • Muse (Meta): reports say its private cloud computer can run recurring jobs and keep working after you close the app. See the Muse guide and check the app for how scheduling works for you.
  • Claude scheduled tasks: in Claude you can create a task that runs on a schedule with its own prompt. Check your plan and settings for availability and limits, and note that some tasks only run while the desktop app is open.
  • ChatGPT Tasks and similar: several chat assistants offer scheduled prompts. Availability depends on the plan, so check the app.

Start free: set up Google Alerts first (5 minutes). Add an AI summary task when you know which topics are worth your time.

How to get started

  1. Choose one topic. Five topics in one summary becomes noise.
  2. Choose how often: daily for fast-moving topics like AI, weekly for slow ones like regulation.
  3. Pick the delivery spot: an email draft, a notification, or the task's own results page.
  4. Copy a prompt below and edit the brackets.
  5. Run it once by hand and read the result. Fix anything that is too long, too vague, or missing sources.
  6. Turn on the schedule. Review after one week and cut what you did not read.

Prompts to copy

Daily AI news

You are my news researcher. Search the web for NEW items from the last 24 hours (go back 3 days only if nothing new turns up) about: 1) major AI model or product launches, 2) AI laws and regulation, 3) new AI tools worth learning. Skip rumors and old news. For each item give: a one-sentence summary in your own words, why it matters for a business student, and a link to the original source. Start with a 2-sentence top takeaway. Keep it under 400 words. If nothing important happened, say so in one line.

Weekly Medicare and insurance changes

Every Monday morning, search for changes in the last 7 days to Medicare Advantage, Medicare Supplement and ACA marketplace rules, CMS announcements, and carrier news in Utah. Use official sources (cms.gov, medicare.gov, state insurance department) first. List each change with the date, a plain-English summary, and the source link. Mark anything that affects what I tell clients as 'VERIFY BEFORE USE'. Do not give advice; only report what the sources say.

Weekly club meeting ideas

Every Wednesday, find 5 new things related to AI that would make a good 2-minute talk at Thursday's club meeting: a new tool, a surprising use case, a business story, a risk or lawsuit, a student-friendly project. For each give the headline, two sentences on why it is interesting, and the source link. Rank them by how well they would spark discussion.

Competitor or keyword watch

Each day, check for news about [COMPANY OR COMPETITOR] and [KEYWORD]. Report only what is new since yesterday: product changes, funding, hiring, pricing, customer complaints, legal issues. For each item add the source link and a one-line 'so what' for a small business. If there is nothing new, reply 'No news today.'

Write a prompt that works

  • Say how recent: "last 24 hours" beats "latest."
  • Demand sources: a link for every item, so you can check it.
  • Set a length: "under 400 words" keeps it readable.
  • Add a no-news rule: otherwise the AI may invent filler.
  • Say what to skip: rumors, old news, duplicates.

Safety rules

  • Open the sources. AI summaries can be wrong. Never act on a summary alone, especially for regulations, money or client advice.
  • Keep it read-only. Tell the task not to send emails or change files unless you approve.
  • No private data. Do not put client names or health details in a scheduled prompt.
  • Web pages can contain tricks. Hidden text on a page may try to give your AI orders (prompt injection). This is another reason to keep tasks read-only.
  • Watch your usage. Each run uses your plan's allowance. Start with one task.

Why it matters for business and IS

Information Systems angle: a scheduled task is automation built from three parts: a trigger (the clock), an action (search and summarize) and an output (the report). The same pattern powers business monitoring, reporting and alerts. The key design question is where a person must stay in the loop.

Business use case

Scenario: A small insurance or consulting firm needs to know when rules or competitors change, but nobody has time to read every source daily.

How it works: A weekly scheduled task searches official sources, lists each change with a link, and flags items for review. A person checks the sources and decides what to tell clients.

Measure success by: Hours saved per week, and the number of changes you caught that you would otherwise have missed.

Watch out for: Wrong or outdated summaries. Always verify with the official source before using it with clients.

Quick facts

  • Google Alerts is free. Scheduled AI tasks depend on your plan, so check the app.
  • Key words: scheduled task, trigger, workflow, prompt injection, human in the loop.
  • AI can miss news or get details wrong, so treat results as leads to check.
